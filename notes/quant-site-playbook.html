Peer survey & implementation order

Quant Site Playbook

What the personal sites of working quants — at Jane Street, HRT, Citadel Securities, Tower, Two Sigma, AQR, Balyasny, Arrowstreet, Headlands, Radix, SIG — actually do, and the order to build it in.

Method · Five parallel crawls, August 2026. Every URL cited was loaded and inspected directly. Sample · ~80 distinct personal sites, plus 20+ hiring-side sources (firm career pages, named practitioners, recruiters). Discovery · GitHub Users API by bio text was the highest-yield channel by far; generic web search returned only interview-prep SEO. Not covered · reddit.com and quantnet.com were inaccessible to the crawlers, so no forum opinion is cited anywhere below.

01

The calibration — read this before building anything

The hiring-side evidence says a personal site matters less than you probably hope. This changes what the site should be, not whether to build it.

The uncomfortable finding

Giuseppe "Gappy" Paleologo — Global Head of Quantitative Research at Balyasny, previously Head of Risk at HRT, Head of Enterprise Risk at Millennium, Director of Equities QR at Citadel, self-described screener of "a couple thousand résumés" — published roughly fifteen pages of buy-side quant career advice in December 2025. It mentions personal websites, blogs, GitHub, and side projects exactly zero times. Not dismissed — absent from the model.

Same zero-mention result on Jane Street's interviewing page and 25-question FAQ, HRT's interviewing post, and the Two Sigma, D. E. Shaw, SIG, and Optiver careers pages.

Even the most project-favourable source found — a writer who landed PM offers at Citadel, Millennium, BlueCrest, Rokos and Brevan largely through personal projects — concedes the consensus verbatim: "most people (including interviewers, practitioners that are way more experienced than me), view personal projects as nothing more than a 'show of interest'." The honest calibration comes from a recruiting-side source: GitHub gets checked "especially when deciding between similar candidates." Tiebreaker, not door-opener.

But the switcher door itself is wide open

This is firm-official, not forum folklore. Every quote below is from a company's own careers material:

  • Jane Street — "Problem-solving mindset: required. Finance background: optional." and "Do I need a background in finance? Nope! We'll teach you everything you need to know."
  • Two Sigma — "more than half of Two Sigma's employees come from outside the finance industry." And a literal posted qualification: "Performed an in-depth research project, examining real-world data."
  • SIG — "The ability to develop a new idea or approach to a problem is far more valued than having a preexisting knowledge of finance." They budget a three-month in-house finance class; finance knowledge is not the scarce input.
  • Optiver — wants people "comfortable reasoning about noisy data, imperfect signals, and risk." That is a description of state-estimation work.
  • HRT — evaluates research on "simplicity, reproducibility, and generality," and writes that "it is easy to lie to ourselves while resolving small differences in our low signal-to-noise domain."

One number worth recalibrating against: D. E. Shaw's posted NYC quantitative analyst base is $275k for UG/MS and $300k for PhD. A $250k target is conservative for the roles you're aiming at.

The reframe

Stop conceiving of the site as lead generation. It is a defensibility instrument — the thing that converts a warm reader from "plausible" to "worth an hour," and the thing that makes you unshakeable when someone probes a claim in an interview.

Its highest-leverage content is not the best strategy you have found. It is documented evidence that you know how easily you could have fooled yourself, and what you did about it. That is the one thing a career switcher can offer that a strong résumé cannot — and per Paleologo's polled managers, per HRT's own blog, and per Feynman via both, it is exactly what the buyers say they want.

Ranked expected value of your channels

  1. Referral or warm introduction into a specific team. Every source points here.
  2. A specific, quantified résumé applied promptly to posted roles. The one artifact universally named.
  3. Specialist recruiter relationships — Selby Jennings, Durlston, H.W. Anderson, Oxford Knight.
  4. Interview readiness — probability, stats, and the ability to defend everything you have ever written down.
  5. The site and GitHub — conversion and tiebreaking. Real, but downstream of 1–4.
  6. Public writing at scale over years — highest ceiling, longest horizon, least reliable on a job-search timeline.

The structural constraint you're under

Across the prop and HFT lane, employer badge and content volume are almost perfectly inversely correlated. This single fact determines which architecture is available to you.

The prop/HFT lane has already solved the problem you're solving, because they face the same constraint for a different reason: they can't publish their employer's work. Their workarounds are directly transferable — move research onto public or crypto data, write about infrastructure rather than alpha, fence pre-employment work behind an explicit date range, and state exactly what you are not claiming.

Critical negative finding

Not one person in the prop/HFT lane publishes an equity curve with a headline Sharpe. In that population it reads as either a compliance violation or an overfit. The only performance-shaped artifact anyone published was a paper-vs-live reconciliation where the divergence is explained mechanistically.

03

Tier 1 — Evidence content

Ranked by signal per unit of effort. These are the things that actually move a quant hiring manager, and they are all content, not design.

1

A visible record of research that failed, framed as normal

Rarest and highest-value signal found, ranked first independently by two lanes. Nobody fabricating results writes these sentences.

Almost all of what I have looked at has been a dead end… most research ends up in the bin. Mark Best · markrbest.github.io
Also: Kinlay discards a metric that gave him the answer he wanted, because no null had been run. Allocate Smartly's entire "Beware Strategies That Fall Down on Good Data" is a demolition of a result they'd have preferred to keep.
Promote post-mortems to a named top-level nav item. Nobody in the prop/HFT lane has one, and your evaluation protocol already commits you to writing them. This is the single clearest differentiator available to you.
2

An explicit "what this does not show" section in every post

Jonathan Kinlay runs this as a fixed section in every article, alongside a second fixed section named "Failure modes I actually saw." Every bullet begins "It does not show that…". Nothing else so cheaply proves you are not selling something.

It does not show that this kind of CNN-based repair is useful on real data. … It does not, on its own, justify a production system. Jonathan Kinlay · "Deep Learning for Volatility Surface Repair"
Make it a literal required heading in your post template. It costs one paragraph and it is the highest trust-per-word content available.
3

Uncertainty attached to every headline number

Kinlay reports 0.0184 ± 0.0010 and states the SE formula (std(ddof=1)/√N). He publishes confidence intervals on correlations: +0.15 [−0.02, +0.37] against +0.82 [+0.67, +0.91]. Abdelmessih wrote an entire post whose purpose was showing a correlation's CI is [+0.02, +0.72]. David Álvarez Rosa leads with the coefficient of variation rather than the mean.

Every number on the site gets a dispersion measure. Your MinTRL treatment already does this for Sharpe — extend the habit to forecast errors, cost estimates, and fit quality.
4

Trial count disclosed, then a deflation applied to it

Almost nobody does this, which is exactly why it lands. The Refutation discloses 55 configs and 320 variants. López de Prado's own warning is the reason it matters: "It typically takes about 20 such iterations to discover a (false) investment strategy" at 5% significance.

Watch this specifically

López de Prado, verbatim: "It does not matter if the backtest is a walk-forward out-of-sample. The fact that we are repeating a test over and over on the same data will likely lead to a false discovery." Your live out-of-sample record is valuable only with the trial count, the pre-registration date, the sample size, and honest error bars. "Out-of-sample" used as a magic word is a red flag, not a defence.

Your protocol already promises automatic trial-count logging. Surface the running count as a live number on the site — a counter that only goes up is unfakeable and nobody else has one.
5

Paper-vs-live reconciliation, with divergence explained mechanistically

The only performance artifact the prop/HFT lane publishes at all. Mark Best's four-part slippage series gives a simulation-vs-paper-vs-live table, and the value is not the number — it's "the forecast takes about 200 ms to calculate on a new bar thus in paper and live the orders are sent later than in sim" and "crypto exchanges lack sequence IDs." Naming the mechanism of your own slippage is the strongest possible evidence you have run a real system.

Frame your live record around divergence explanation rather than returns. Backtest curve, hard vertical line at go-live, realized path after it, and the gap quantified and attributed.
6

A standing "this analysis may contain errors" disclaimer

The most transferable single artifact in the entire survey, three clauses long:

Kipp Rogers began studying market data in his free time, later founding Anamnesis LLC… The contents of this blog are purely his opinions, do not represent the views of Anamnesis LLC, are the products of analysis which may have errors, and do not constitute any sort of trading or investment advice. Kipp Rogers · mechanicalmarkets.wordpress.com

Paired with a per-post invitation: "Please feel free to discuss anything that looks wrong in the comments section or over email with me."

Costs nothing, buys enormous credibility with an audience whose job is scrutinising claims. Add Carver's companion line too — explicitly not regulated, not selling courses or signals.
7

Exact data provenance, with defects acknowledged

Markwick names the exact API and its ten-year history limit, then enumerates all 59 ETFs in the universe. Kinlay discloses "synthetic only" up front. Allocate Smartly explains precisely when its simulated pre-ETF data is invalid. Vague provenance reads as sloppiness or concealment.

Vendor, instruments, exact date range, how it was obtained, what you had to pay for or proxy, and what's wrong with it. Your data_ingest_infra repo already says "Polygon" — put that on the site.
8

A named baseline you compare against — and sometimes lose to

Kinlay benchmarks his CNN against plain interpolation and reports that the gap is small and may not survive real data. That's the clearest tell separating real research from a backtest.

Your protocol already names HAR-RV, GARCH, implied-as-forecast, and naive short carry. Publish a result where one of them wins.
9

Replication of a named paper, with the delta reported

Markwick replicates Nick Baltas's Cross-Asset Skew, cites it, credits the podcast where he heard about it, and says plainly: "the results are not as conclusive as the original paper."

This is the safest possible first post — it proves competence with zero risk of an unfalsifiable claim, and it ships before your own system has results. Candidates: a Bakshi–Kapadia–Madan variance-risk-premium replication, or Carr–Madan variance-swap replication under real option chains.
10

Infrastructure writing as a safe competence proxy

Latency, logging, OMS design, reconciliation, benchmark methodology. It proves engineering seriousness, is fully verifiable by the reader, and contains zero alpha. Four separate people in the prop/HFT lane lean on it.

Your structured logging, daily reconciliation, and silent-failure alerting are not boring side-details — they are the most publishable thing you have that carries no claim risk. Write them up before you have any results at all.
11

Open-source code that somebody else uses

Third-party adoption is unfakeable and IP-free. The best eleven words in the survey come from Ties de Kok describing his own package: "Officially integrated into Stata as of Stata 17." Sepp maintains qis, stochvolmodels, optimalportfolios; Markwick maintains HawkesProcesses.jl; Letian Wang ships quanttrader on PyPI.

Extract one genuinely reusable piece — an SVI/SSVI arbitrage-free fitter, or a deflated-Sharpe/PBO implementation — and ship it as a named, installable package.
12

Downstream evaluation, not just metric improvement

Kinlay checks whether repaired surfaces produce better SVI fits, not merely lower RMSE.

Does a better vol forecast produce better P&L after hedging costs — not just lower MSE? Answer that in public and you've demonstrated the thing most ML-into-finance candidates never grasp.
04

Tier 2 — Positioning & structure

13

Lead with a falsifiable market thesis, not a self-description

Volatility regimes migrate across asset classes, and models that feel robust fail at the worst moment. I build frameworks designed to survive regime change. Artur Sepp · Risk Magazine Quant of the Year 2024

You have no fund name to lead with, so a thesis is the only substitute — and it's the better asset anyway, because it's checkable.

Your "Options are a market in forecasts. The trade is the forecast error" is already the right shape. It's buried on /vol/; it belongs on the homepage above the biography.
14

Isomorphism in sentence one

Two Sigma's own switcher stories all turn on the same move — "the basic problem set is very similar" (Rob, from the intelligence community), "let's do a similar thing in finance" (Mike, ex-Google). Noisy sensor state estimation → noisy, non-stationary market signal.

Your Lockheed particle-filter line already does this well. Push it higher and use Optiver's own words back at them: "reasoning about noisy data, imperfect signals, and risk."
15

Depth in one named sub-specialty, visible at a glance

The unit of credibility is a thread, not a portfolio. Markwick = Hawkes processes and execution; Sepp = log-normal stochastic volatility. Neither says "quantitative finance."

The counterexample proves it: Peter Cotton's site indexes eighteen research domains, each with papers and shipped software, and is genuinely more accomplished than most sites in the survey — but a hiring manager scanning it cannot state what he does.

Your capability tag clouds (three groups, 26 tags) are the flat-breadth pattern. Cut them hard, or replace them with the one thread you want to be known for.
16

Give the system a name, a repo, and a version

Everyone credible has named artifacts: Sepp's ROSAA and his log-normal beta SV model, López de Prado's purged CV / deflated Sharpe / meta-labeling, Markwick's HawkesProcesses.jl. Once a method has your name on it, breadth elsewhere becomes free.

"The volatility system" cannot be cited, starred, or referenced by anyone else. Name it, version it, and make it the thing your posts are about.
17

A boring five-item nav that separates kinds of evidence

Best information architecture in the survey is Ties de Kok's: About me · Research · Code & Data · Talks & Classes · Blog Posts · CV. A hiring manager wants to jump straight to the type of evidence they trust, and each nav item should be exactly one type.

Yours is Volatility System / Research Log / CV / Contact. The gap: Code & Data and Post-mortems as their own destinations.
18

Abstracts inline — assume 45 seconds and no clicks

De Kok expands full abstracts on the research page. Abdelmessih puts a one-line finding under every post title ("Futures lead the cash"). Chinco's publication entries each carry a plain-language findings paragraph. Mark Best's feed shows 60–80 word excerpts.

Nothing on your site should require a click to evaluate. Every log entry gets its finding — not its topic — visible in the listing.
19

Fence pre-employment and non-proprietary work with an explicit date range

Nick Georgakopoulos heads his section "Math Papers and Code (2017–2022)"; Sunny Balasubramanian writes "Formerly at Citadel, AQR." Signalling that you know where the line is is itself the signal that you can be trusted near proprietary information.

State plainly that the vol system is built entirely on your own time with purchased retail data, and contains nothing from any employer.
20

Group by theme once the archive passes ~10 items — never by year alone

A chronological blog decays; a themed index compounds. Newfound names its research programs (Liquidity Cascades, Rebalance Timing Luck); Moontower uses topic clusters; Qiwen Cui groups publications under four research themes so a reader sees four coherent programs rather than eleven scattered papers.

05

Tier 3 — Research-post craft

21

Byline and date on every post — non-negotiable

The Hull Tactical blog, where Euan Sinclair writes, is active, expert, and carries no author attribution on any post. Unattributed research accrues to nobody. Meanwhile three otherwise-credible reference sites in the survey are 2–3.5 years stale, which reads worse than having no research section at all.

If you can't sustain a cadence, present a fixed set of finished pieces with no dates implying a schedule. A visibly abandoned log is worse than no log.
22

Numbered, captioned figures referenced in the prose

Gundersen bounds every figure with hairline rules and a bold Figure N. label. Newfound puts a bold caption above and a standardized source line below — "Calculations by …". Cheap, and it does more to make a page read as research than any other single change.

23

Booktabs tables — horizontal rules only

Gundersen: 2px top rule, 1px bottom, 1px under the header, no vertical rules anywhere. Vertical rules and zebra striping read as spreadsheet export, not analysis.

24

Real math, rendered — pre-rendered at build time

Math rendering is present on essentially every serious site in the prop/HFT lane; its absence on a quant site is a negative signal, and a broken CDN reference is worse than nothing. Gundersen's Black–Scholes post contains 186 pre-rendered <span class="katex"> blocks and zero math JavaScript at runtime.

Render KaTeX at build time in your existing build_site.py, ship katex.min.css only, self-hosted, one version.
25

Real citations to primary sources, with anchors

Gundersen carries six properly formatted author-year citations on a single blog post, with anchor IDs. Kinlay cites Bailey & López de Prado, Harvey–Liu–Zhu, White's Reality Check, and McLean & Pontiff inline. Kipp Rogers grounds the square-root impact law in published empirics across three asset classes.

Cite the specific results, not the names. "Sinclair and López de Prado" as influences is weaker than one correctly formatted reference to Bailey & López de Prado (2014) on the deflated Sharpe ratio.
26

Reproducible code tied to the specific figure

Ranked ordering from the survey: linked repo at an exact commit > inline snippet > nothing > a screenshot of code. Kinlay ships an ~800-line self-contained script headed "CPU-friendly" — a deliberate reproducibility signal. Markwick's Julia is complete and runnable inside the post.

27

Costs stated as specific numbers

Allocate Smartly: "0.10% per trade / 0.20% round-trip," executed at the 4pm close. Carver reports his actual slippage bill and what his execution algo saved — 78bp, about 40%. For an options system: bid/ask, exchange fees, and delta-hedge slippage, quantified rather than waved at.

Tier 4 — Mechanics & discoverability

Verdict on your stack

Stay where you are. Do not migrate. Hand-written HTML on GitHub Pages with a custom domain puts you at or above the median of the entire survey, and ahead of nearly all of it on structured data. Your hand-rolled inline SVG charts beat both Plotly bloat and static PNGs. No Vercel, Astro, or Next.js appears anywhere in the quant cohort — Jekyll dominates, Quarto is the one validated notebook path.

Two calibration points worth internalizing before spending another hour on design: Peter Jäckel — author of "Let's Be Rational," the implied-vol inversion algorithm the industry runs on — publishes hand-written HTML containing IE6 workarounds and a flat list of forty PDFs. López de Prado's own homepage is a 357-byte <frameset> served from IIS. Campbell Harvey's 176-paper research page is Netscape-era framesets. Bryan Kelly, Head of ML at AQR, is on Wix. Lasse Pedersen, an AQR principal, is on Google Sites.

28

Sitemap, robots.txt, and an RSS/Atom feed

A feed is how Quantocracy — still the best live aggregator in this niche — picks you up, which puts you in the same daily roundup as Carver and Kinlay. It's the cheapest real distribution available.

29

Make your GitHub profile carry the same positioning as the site

The highest-yield discovery channel in the entire survey was the GitHub Users API filtered by bio text and location:"New York". That's a query a technically-minded recruiter genuinely runs — and your profile currently has no bio, no location, no website field, no profile README, and two near-duplicate repos (data_ingest_infra and data_ingestion_infra).

Bio, location: New York, NY, blog: bradlasater.com, a profile README linking the system and the log, descriptions on every repo, and delete the duplicate. Your site says "documented in the open" and links "Source" to a bare profile.
30

Write the meta description in the third person, naming city and specialty

Gundersen's is the model: "Gregory Gundersen is a quantitative researcher in New York." Sunny Balasubramanian's names his prior employers outright — deliberate recruiter-SEO and the single most copyable move in that lane. Search engines surface the description verbatim, so it's the one sentence a recruiter is most likely to read before deciding whether to click.

31

Ship both CV formats

Recruiters forward PDFs and applicant-tracking systems ingest PDFs; hiring managers read HTML. You have the HTML; add /cv.pdf.

32

Protect the domain — it's a decaying asset

Two reference sites in this survey have already been lost. frouah.com, the standard free reference for Heston derivations, and kthohr.com, an NYU PhD's site, both lapsed and now serve online-casino spam under their owners' names. A lapsed registration doesn't go quiet — it gets bought because it has inbound links.

Auto-renew plus registrar lock on bradlasater.com, and never retire the bradlasater.github.io fallback, which cannot be seized.
33

Self-host the fonts and finish the analytics

Google Fonts is a render-blocking third-party request; the serious sites either self-host or use a system stack. Separately, your GoatCounter CODE is still empty, so nothing is being measured — you currently have no idea whether anyone reads this.

07

The post template

Derived by merging Kinlay's fixed skeleton with the conventions recurring across the vol lane. Two of these sections essentially never appear on retail sites, which is exactly why they work. 

<Specific, falsifiable title — a claim or a question, never a topic>
Byline · Date · Read time

## The question
  The decision it affects. The prior you held going in.

## Data
  Source named exactly. Universe enumerated. N. Exclusions.
  Known defects. Point-in-time discipline.

## Method
  Reimplementable by a reader. Every parameter and how it was chosen.
  Variants tested before this one: N.
  Named baselines. Costs as numbers, not adjectives.

## Results
  Headline number ± SE or CI. Tables over prose.
  ≤2 figures: bold caption above, source line below.
  Median and quartiles, not only the mean.

## Robustness
  OOS split date chosen before fitting. Multiple IS/OOS windows.
  An out-of-distribution family. Bootstrap.
  Deflated Sharpe / PBO-CSCV with the trial count.
  ±30% parameter perturbation. Opposite-rule sign test.

## What this shows and does not show          ← non-negotiable
  Every bullet begins "It does not show that…"

## Failure modes I actually saw
  Name your own model's weaknesses before a reader finds them.

## What I'd do next
  Ranked. Cheapest decisive test first.

## Code & references
  The exact commit that produced every figure. Runtime and hardware.
  Real author-year citations with anchors.

Length: 1,800–4,500 words is the observed norm. Kipp Rogers runs 3,000–7,000. Register: first person, about decisions and tradeoffs — never third-person marketing copy.

08

The track-record spec

Rob Carver's twelve-year public series is the reference implementation. Almost nobody else in the survey has a live record at all — which makes yours a genuine differentiator, provided it carries this genre's caveat conventions. Without them it looks like every other backtest.

What Carver does, in order

  1. Leads with the worst number. His year-11 post opens "it's my worst ever." A track record that only surfaces in good years is not a track record.
  2. States the exact accounting basis. Mark-to-market and net-of-everything, side by side, with the currency and the period boundaries named.
  3. Decomposes the P&L. −16.3% total = −14.5% futures + −0.64% cash ETFs + −2.7% FX, then by market and by trading rule. Decomposition is the strongest anti-cherry-picking signal available, because it exposes every component including the losers.
  4. Benchmarks against named third-party indices, with correlations. SG CTA Index and AHL at 0.68 and 0.56 — which simultaneously proves he isn't levered beta and situates the result in the peer distribution.
  5. Reports costs as a first-class result. "Without my execution algo I would have paid 1.34% in slippage; my simple algo earned 78bp and cut the bill by around 40%."
  6. Deducts the risk-free rate, or says he didn't.
  7. Shows the full path, not a summary statistic.
  8. Admits discretionary errors by name. "(yes, bad move in retrospect, and another failure of discretionary trading on my part)."
Four gaps even Carver leaves open

These are genuine differentiation, not table stakes — nobody in the survey does them:

  • A live-vs-backtest overlay. Backtest equity curve, a hard vertical line at go-live, the realized path after it, and the gap quantified.
  • A confidence interval on your own realized Sharpe. SE(SR) ≈ √((1 + SR²/2)/N). On eighteen months it will be humiliatingly wide. Publishing it anyway is the single most credible thing you can put on that page.
  • A frozen, pre-registered definition. "Parameters were frozen on <date>; config hash <hash>; nothing below has been re-fit." Verifiable against your git history — which your CI already makes tamper-evident.
  • A change log. Every intervention, override, and parameter change, dated, with the reason. Silence about changes reads as changes concealed.
How it goes wrong

Observed in the survey, all of it disqualifying with this audience:

  • A dashboard with no methodology behind it, or the methodology paywalled.
  • Cumulative return only — no vol, no drawdown, no benchmark, no costs.
  • Headline Sharpe with no interval and no trial count. One demonstration in the survey shows a 0.39 daily Sharpe deflating below zero once 100 trials are accounted for.
  • Gross-of-costs presented as live. Any results section that doesn't state its cost assumption is assumed to have none.
  • Restarted or re-based records that don't disclose what came before.
09

Phrasing conventions

Scope limitation — use these constructions literally

"It does not show that ___ is useful on real data."
"It does not show that ___ is the right architecture for this task."
"It does not, on its own, justify a production system."
"Whether ___ survives a real-data test is an open question."
"The synthetic surfaces have no calibration drift, no quote-time-of-day noise, no microstructure asymmetries."

Calibrated confidence

"I would not defend it further than 'worth running properly.'"
"Should be read as an order of magnitude, not a point estimate."
"Treat the ordering as the result and the levels as approximate."
"This is a small one, and it does not come out where I expected."
"Necessary, but not sufficient."

Naming your own failure modes

"The heteroscedastic head undercovers."
"The convergence is slow and the validation curve is still improving slightly at epoch 60."
"We have neglected the trading costs and potential capacity… that is a problem to solve for another day."

Reporting a null, and steelmanning against yourself

"I'm taking the absence of any contradictory evidence as evidence that I should continue to do what I've done before."
"We unfortunately can't reject the null hypothesis."
"Is it possible that a weak edge existed but was hidden by imperfections in the data? Absolutely."
"The test confidently says 'nothing to see here.' It's technically correct."

Delete on sight

proven consistently robust (unqualified) proprietary cutting-edge passionate about significant returns battle-tested alpha-generating any Sharpe without a period, a cost assumption, and an interval

10

Anti-patterns

Ranked by how much damage each does with this specific audience. The first four are disqualifying in a way that having no number at all is not.

  1. A headline return or Sharpe with no trial count. Read as evidence of not knowing that trials must be controlled for — which is worse than publishing nothing.
  2. "Out-of-sample" used as a magic word without pre-registration date, sample size, and error bars.
  3. Implausible numbers. One live site in the survey advertises "250%+ annual returns (pre-costs)" and a "1.7 Sharpe Ratio" with no methodology.
  4. Results with no cost, slippage, or capacity model. Instantly visible to a vol person.
  5. No risk section. A vol strategy with no drawdown behaviour, tail exposure, gap risk, or stated failure conditions reads as naive about short-vol payoffs specifically.
  6. "AI trading bot" framing, or complexity presented as the achievement. HRT: "an incremental 0.1% improvement in the accuracy of a neural network's ability to distinguish dog breeds might not translate to predicting the price of a stock."
  7. Skills walls and technology laundry lists. "Interviewers will pick the most obscure item on your list." Same applies to a badge grid of libraries.
  8. Anything you cannot defend for twenty minutes. Naming "combinatorially symmetric cross-validation" without having implemented it is worse than not mentioning it.
  9. Vague accomplishment language. "'Developed quantitative models' tells a recruiter nothing. Which models? What data? What was the result?"
  10. Design investment visibly exceeding research investment. The best-designed site in the hedge-fund lane was the least technically credible.
  11. A bare artifact dump with no framing — and its inverse, a beautiful landing page with nothing behind it. One site in the survey has genuinely strong volatility research behind a landing page that reads "Notebook Reports" plus six bare .html links. No name, no bio, no findings.
  12. Burying the transferable work. State estimation and real-time optimization must be surfaced and translated into market vocabulary, or they read as generic ML résumé filler.
  13. Overclaiming the identity. "Engineer building a volatility research program" is defensible. "Quantitative researcher," on a site with no desk experience, invites a comparison you lose.
  14. Non-work content in the primary nav, mixed-topic dilution, and undated content.
  15. Dormancy. Worse than having no research section, because it reads as an abandoned project.

What not to build

Effort you would be spending against the evidence. Each of these was checked across the whole sample.

Don'tEvidence from the survey
A dark theme Across ~80 sites, essentially one ships a real toggle, and not one site in the hedge-fund lane implements dark mode at all. Every serious research site is light-only with a near-grayscale palette. Your dark-only design system is the outlier — spend the effort on the light palette instead.
A JS charting library Exactly one site out of twenty uses interactive charts; it cost them ten scripts and thirteen stylesheets on a landing page. Charts in this field are overwhelmingly static images with numbered captions. Your hand-rolled SVG is the better middle path.
Migrating to Astro / Next / Vercel None of them appear anywhere in the quant cohort. Jekyll dominates; Quarto is the one validated notebook path, and only if you later want .ipynb publishing in a subdirectory.
Hosted search Your site is far too small. Nobody at this scale has it.
A CSS framework or SPA Not one site in the hedge-fund lane uses a JS framework for the site itself. Two sites in the prop lane render literally nothing without JavaScript — an unforced total failure.
More visual polish generally Three top-tier people's sites aren't mobile-responsive at all, and one deliberately loads non-responsive.css. Design is not the binding constraint for this audience — and visible design effort exceeding research effort is itself a negative signal.
Two things to fix in what you already have

There is a TODO(brad) comment shipped in your homepage source, flagging that the "roughly 60%" Ticketmaster forecast-error claim doesn't name its metric or baseline. The TODO's instinct is right — that's the first number a quant interviewer pushes on — but it currently reads as a public note-to-self admitting the claim is unqualified. Name the metric, name the baseline, or cut the number.
