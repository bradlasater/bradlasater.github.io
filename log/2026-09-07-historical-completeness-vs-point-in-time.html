September 7, 2026 Data & cleaning

Half my datasets can be backfilled and half cannot, and that decides what a replay may claim

Option day bars reach back to 2022-08-31 and can be rebuilt from the vendor archive at any time; chain snapshots exist only from 2026-08-31 because there is no historical snapshot endpoint. That asymmetry, not storage cost, is what limits which backtests this system will ever be allowed to run.

The question

Every dataset in this warehouse looks the same once it has landed: partitioned Parquet under one root, queried the same way. That similarity is misleading. Some of these datasets can be reconstructed from the vendor at any point in the future, and some exist only because a process was running at a particular moment and wrote them down. If I lose the second kind, it is gone permanently — there is no endpoint that will sell it back to me.

The question this entry settles is which is which, and what each category is allowed to support in a backtest.

What I expected

I expected the split to fall along the usual lines: recent data cheap and available, older data expensive and available. The plan I started with assumed I could backfill the option-chain snapshots the same way I backfill trades and bars, and that the only cost of starting the capture later would be a gap I could fill in afterwards.

What happened

The datasets separate into four categories, not two, and the boundary is availability rather than age.

  • Irreplaceable. option_snapshots and the parity forwards derived from them. There is no historical snapshot endpoint on this vendor at any tier. The archive begins on 2026-08-31, the first capture day, because that is the day the sweep started running. A day not captured is a day that does not exist.
  • Backfillable. Option trades, minute bars and day bars, all of which can be pulled from the vendor's flat-file archive on demand. option_day_bars reach back to 2022-08-31. When the websocket capture and the flat file disagree, the flat file wins and the reconciliation job rewrites the local copy.
  • Write-only. contracts_expired and underlying_day_bars. Captured on a schedule, consumed by nothing yet. Worth capturing anyway precisely because the first category exists: the cost of storing something I do not need is bounded, and the cost of not having captured it is not.
  • Derived. The ATM term structure, rebuildable from the day bars in about seven minutes. Nothing here needs protecting; it needs only to be reproducible.

The four-year gap between those two dates — 2022-08-31 for day bars, 2026-08-31 for snapshots — is the whole finding.

Diagnosis

A snapshot is a statement about what the market looked like at an instant, including which contracts existed, their strikes and expiries, and their open interest. That is a fact about a moment, and a vendor that did not record it cannot manufacture it later. A day bar is an aggregate over a session, computed from a trade tape that is itself archived, so it can be recomputed indefinitely.

The practical consequence is that the richer dataset has the shorter history, and it always will. Every additional day of snapshot history costs one day of waiting and cannot be bought. Meanwhile the poorer dataset — day bars, carrying no contract terms at all, with the strike and expiry recovered by parsing the OPRA symbol — is the only one long enough to fit anything to.

This is also why a coverage audit that reports only on job failures is insufficient. A job that stops running produces no failures at all, and for an irreplaceable dataset the loss is permanent by the time anybody notices. The audit therefore asserts what should have landed for a given session — sweep counts derived from the market calendar rather than hardcoded, so early closes are handled — and fails when the observed count falls short. Two real holes were found this way and neither had produced a single error line: an SPX-shaped gap in the trades watchlist, and a websocket job that had never once written a file.

What this means for a replay, and what it forbids

A backtest is a claim that the system would have made these decisions with the information available at the time. Which claims this warehouse can support:

  • Supported from 2022-08-31: anything that needs only at-the-money level and slope per expiry. That is what the term structure carries, and it is genuinely what a 5–45 day book is traded off.
  • Supported from 2026-08-31 only: anything needing the full chain at an instant — open-interest filters, strike-space selection, anything off-strike.
  • Not supported at any date: anything needing a bid, an ask, a mid or displayed depth. Option NBBO is not entitled on this tier. This is a separate limit from the two above and it does not improve with waiting; it improves with money.

The trap I am most concerned about is the second category quietly borrowing the first category's history. A model developed on four years of term structure, then evaluated with a filter that needs open interest, has silently restricted itself to two weeks of data while appearing to use four years.

What changed as a result

Three things. The snapshot sweep is now the job whose failure is treated most seriously, because it is the only one whose output cannot be recovered. A weekly history audit walks every weekday since 2022-08-31 and distinguishes a market holiday from a genuine hole by asking the vendor rather than by consulting a hardcoded calendar. And datasets that nothing currently consumes are captured anyway and labelled write-only, rather than being switched off as waste.

The evaluation consequence is a rule I am fixing now, before there is any result that would make it inconvenient: every backtest states the earliest date its inputs actually support, and that date is derived from the scarcest input it touches, not the most abundant.
